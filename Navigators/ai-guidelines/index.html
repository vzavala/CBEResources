Generative AI Guidelines for CBE Students

This guide is about generative AI, mainly large language models. That means chatbots and assistants that write text or code, such as ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, Google Gemini and Claude. It does not cover other AI tools, such as the machine learning, optimization or simulation methods you learn and use in CBE courses and research. Those follow your course's normal rules. In this guide, "AI" means generative AI. How the terms relate →
UW policy a university rule you must follow Recommended advice for using AI well, not a rule

Only items marked UW policy are university rules. Everything else in this guide is a recommendation from the CBE department. Your instructor's course rules always apply.

Must UW policy

  • Follow each course's AI rules, for every assignment
  • Never enter protected data (others' work or grades, personal info, unpublished research) into AI tools
  • Use only UW NetID tools for non-public course materials
  • You're responsible for everything you submit, including AI mistakes

Recommended

  • Verify AI output with engineering judgment (units, balances, limits)
  • Disclose and cite AI use when it's allowed
  • Use AI to learn, not to skip the learning
  • Keep your prompts or chat history for graded work

Ask

  • Rules unclear? Ask your instructor before you use AI
  • Not sure if data is protected? Assume it is and ask
  • Misconduct process questions: the Office of Student Conduct & Community Standards

Pick a topic

Can I use AI on this?

  1. Check the syllabus, Canvas and the assignment instructions for an AI statement.
  2. If AI is prohibited, or the rules don't mention AI and you're unsure: don't use it. Ask your instructor first.
  3. If AI is allowed: use it only in the ways the instructor permits (e.g., brainstorming, debugging, grammar).
  4. Check your data: is anything you'd paste in protected? (See the data table below.)
  5. Use a UW NetID tool for any non-public course material.
  6. Verify the output and disclose your use as the course requires.

University policy: what is required UW policy

Academic integrity (UW System student conduct code, Chapter 14)

  • Instructors set the AI rules for their course and each assignment. Rules differ between courses.
  • Using AI when it isn't allowed can be academic misconduct: "use of unauthorized materials," Chapter 14, section 14.03(1)(b).
  • You're responsible for the honest completion and representation of your work, including proper citation.
  • If an instructor suspects unauthorized AI use, they will meet with you, as in any misconduct case.
  • Not allowed unless your instructor explicitly permits it: submitting AI-generated answers, lab reports or code as your own, and using AI on take-home exams. Letting AI fabricate data, results or references is never allowed.

Official source: Office of Student Conduct: generative AI and academic integrity · Academic misconduct

Using AI well in CBE Recommended

Examples in CBE courses (when your instructor allows AI)

Usually fine (if allowed)

  • Asking for a different explanation of a concept (fugacity in CBE 310/311, boundary layers in CBE 320)
  • Generating extra practice problems to test yourself before an exam
  • Asking for a hint on setting up a material balance in CBE 250, then finishing the problem yourself
  • Checking grammar and clarity in a lab report you wrote (CBE 324/424)

Not OK

  • Pasting a homework problem and submitting the AI's solution when AI is prohibited
  • Having AI write the discussion section of a lab report without permission
  • Uploading a teammate's report, someone's grades, or co-op employer documents
  • Accepting AI numbers without checking. AI often gets units, signs and property data wrong.
  • Using AI on exams or quizzes unless explicitly allowed
  • Using AI to debug code in CBE 255, except on the specific problems where it is allowed. Debugging is a skill you build there yourself.

Using AI well as a chemical engineer: connect it back to your course

AI, a classmate or office hours can get you past one problem. Exams and later courses test the connections between problems, so close the loop every time.

  1. Find where it was covered. After a hint, look up the topic in your lecture notes, the textbook and earlier homework.
  2. Name the principle. Which balance, law or assumption does this problem use? Where else have you used it?
  3. Try a cousin problem. Solve a similar problem from class or the textbook without any help.
  4. Explain it in your own words. If you can't explain it without the AI's text, you haven't learned it yet.

Official source: UW–Madison Libraries: using generative AI in your coursework

Verify like an engineer

  • Check units and dimensions, and run mass and energy balances on the result.
  • Test limiting cases (zero flow, infinite time, ideal behavior) and compare with textbook or lecture results.
  • Confirm property data and equations against authoritative sources (textbook, the national property databases, course notes). AI can invent references.

Disclose and cite

Sample AI-use statement (adapt to your course)

"I used Microsoft Copilot Chat (Nov 2026) to explain the McCabe–Thiele method and to debug my Python plotting code. I wrote all analysis and text myself and checked the results against Example 8.3 in the course notes. Prompts are available on request."

For citation formats, see UW Libraries: Citing generative AI.

In research labs

  • Follow your research advisor's rules. Don't upload unpublished data, manuscripts, or proprietary or human-subject data unless your research advisor confirms an approved tool and use. Research with human subjects also falls under the Institutional Review Board.
  • AI use in writing can affect authorship, intellectual property and confidentiality. Talk with your research advisor before using AI for papers, posters or theses.

Build your AI skills Recommended

Use these only in courses or assignments where AI is allowed, or on your own practice problems. Using AI well is a professional skill, and like any skill it takes practice.

Write a good prompt: five ingredients

ContextWho you are and what you're working on: "I'm a junior in a transport phenomena course…"
TaskExactly what you want: explain, quiz me, check, compare, or outline.
ConstraintsLimits and assumptions: "steady state, no reaction, SI units, don't give the final answer."
FormatHow to answer: "step by step, list every equation with units, at most 200 words."
CheckAsk it to expose its reasoning: "state your assumptions and how I could verify this."

Weak prompt

"Solve this heat exchanger problem."

Strong prompt

"I'm studying for CBE 326. Without solving it for me, explain how to choose between the LMTD and effectiveness–NTU methods for a counter-current exchanger where both outlet temperatures are unknown. List the equations with units and the assumptions behind them, then give me one practice question to check my understanding."

Useful phrases

"Give me a hint, not the answer.""Ask me questions before you answer.""Explain step by step and state your assumptions." "List every equation with its units.""Check my solution for errors, but don't fix them. Tell me where to look.""What would make this answer wrong?" "Explain this at the level of a sophomore course.""Give sources I can verify, and say if you're unsure.""Quiz me with three questions, one at a time."

AI challenges: practice with CBE problems

🔎 Catch the hallucinationAsk an AI tool for a compound's vapor-pressure (Antoine) constants and source. Check them in a trusted property database or your textbook. Did it get the numbers, the units or the reference wrong?
🎓 Tutor, not answer keyHave the AI quiz you on CBE 250 material balances one question at a time, giving hints only. Afterwards, write down what you couldn't do without help.
🐞 Debug, don't outsourceIn a course that allows it, give the AI your broken code and ask only where the bug is. Fix it yourself, and explain the fix in one sentence. (Not in CBE 255, where you learn to debug on your own.)
📏 Units and balances auditAsk the AI to solve a textbook energy-balance problem you've already solved. Check every unit and close the balance. Where did it go wrong, if anywhere?
↔️ Test the limitsAsk how an answer behaves at limiting cases (zero flow, infinite time, ideal mixture). Does the AI's result reduce to what you'd expect?
⚖️ Compare and critiqueAsk two approved tools the same conceptual question (e.g., why fugacity matters in phase equilibrium). Compare the answers against your notes, and decide which is better and why.

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms used in these guidelines.

Artificial intelligenceAny computer system doing tasks that need human-like intelligence, including search, optimization and expert systems
Machine learningLearns patterns from data (e.g., predicting reaction yields)
Deep learningMany-layered neural networks
Generative AICreates new text, code, images or molecules
Large language modelsText-based generative AI behind chatbots

Why it matters: "AI" and "large language model" are not synonyms. A chatbot is one kind of generative AI. Most AI in chemical engineering (process models, property prediction, control and optimization) is not a chatbot at all. In these guidelines, "AI tools" means generative AI tools unless stated otherwise.

Artificial intelligence (AI)
Computer systems that perform tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as recognizing patterns, making predictions, understanding language or making decisions.
Machine learning
A branch of AI where computers learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. ChemE example: predicting a mixture's properties or a catalyst's activity from past experiments.
Deep learning / neural networks
Machine learning models built from many connected layers that can learn complex patterns in images, text, sound or molecular structures.
Generative AI
AI that creates new content (text, code, images, even molecules) based on patterns learned from large amounts of data. Copilot, Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude are examples.
Large language model
The engine behind AI chatbots: a model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word. It produces fluent answers, but it doesn't check facts the way a person would.
AI tool vs. model
The model is the engine. The tool or product (a chat app, a plug-in) wraps it with an interface and data protections. The same model can be safe in one tool and unsafe in another.
Prompt
The question, instructions or material you type or upload into an AI tool. Treat a prompt in a consumer tool like a public post.
Hallucination
When AI states something false with confidence: wrong numbers, invented equations, fake references. This is why verification is required.
Training data
The data a model learned from. It may be outdated, incomplete or biased, and some consumer tools also learn from what users type.
AI agent
An AI system that can take actions on its own (run code, search, edit files) to reach a goal, with limited human supervision.
UW-approved tool
An AI service UW–Madison provides with a NetID login and contract protections for university data, such as Microsoft Copilot Chat or Google Gemini.
Consumer tool
A personal or free AI account (e.g., free ChatGPT or a personal Claude account). It has no UW data protections, even if you sign in with your NetID.
Data classification
UW's system for labeling data by risk (Public, Internal, Sensitive, Restricted; becoming Low, Moderate and High risk in 2027). It decides which data may go into which tools.
Protected data
Data that may not go into AI tools without review, e.g., student records and grades, health information, unpublished research and confidential material.
Academic misconduct
Dishonesty in academic work. Using AI where it isn't allowed counts as "use of unauthorized materials" under the UW System student conduct code (Chapter 14).
AI-use statement (disclosure)
A short note saying which AI tool you used, what for, and how you checked the results. Many courses require one when AI is allowed.
AI detector
Software that claims to identify AI-written text. It is unreliable and biased against non-native English writers, and UW–Madison does not provide one.
Verification
Checking AI output against reliable sources and engineering judgment: units, mass and energy balances, limiting cases, textbook values.
