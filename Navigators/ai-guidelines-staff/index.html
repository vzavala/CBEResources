Generative AI Guidelines for CBE Staff

This guide is about generative AI at work, mainly large language models. That means chatbots and assistants that write text, summarize documents or analyze spreadsheets, such as Microsoft Copilot, Google Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude. It is written for CBE administrative staff: finance, payroll and benefits, administration, the undergraduate and graduate programs, communications, and IT. In this guide, "AI" means generative AI. How the terms relate →
UW policy a university rule you must follow Recommended advice for using AI well, not a rule

Only items marked UW policy are university rules. Everything else in this guide is a recommendation from the CBE department. Your supervisor's and your office's own procedures always apply.

Must UW policy

  • Keep sensitive data (student records, personal info, IDs, health, personnel matters) out of AI tools
  • Use only UW NetID tools for internal work documents
  • Follow your department's AI rules too
  • You're responsible for anything you send, sign or submit, including AI mistakes
  • Report a possible data exposure right away

Recommended

  • Check every number, name, date and amount before you use it
  • Remove names and indirect identifiers, or use made-up data, when you try something new
  • Start with tedious, repetitive tasks
  • Keep a person in charge of decisions, approvals and signatures

Ask

  • Not sure if data is sensitive? Assume it is and ask
  • Not sure a tool is approved? Check it.wisc.edu/ai or the DoIT Help Desk
  • Want to try AI on a team task? Talk to your supervisor first

Pick a topic

Can I use AI on this task?

  1. Look at the data. What would you paste or upload? Find its level in the data table (next topic).
  2. Sensitive or restricted data? Stop. Remove names, IDs and amounts tied to people, or don't use AI for this task.
  3. Internal data (drafts, procedures, schedules, department budgets): use only a UW NetID tool such as Microsoft Copilot Chat or Google Gemini.
  4. Public data (published pages, public course listings): any tool is fine, but a UW tool is still the better habit.
  5. Check before you use it. Verify the output yourself. AI drafts; you decide, approve and sign.
  6. Check your department's rules. UW employees must also follow their unit's AI rules. Unsure? Ask your supervisor.
Quick rule

If you wouldn't post it on the department website, it goes only into a UW NetID tool. If you wouldn't email it to a colleague in another department, it doesn't go into AI at all.

Working with sensitive data UW policy Recommended

Much of our work touches information that would hurt students, colleagues or the department if it leaked. AI can still help with that work, as long as the sensitive part never goes in.

Never paste or upload UW policy

  • Student names together with grades, ID numbers, advising notes, financial aid, disability or conduct information
  • Social Security, bank, card or account numbers, including on scanned receipts and forms
  • Health, leave or accommodation information, and personnel or performance matters
  • Passwords, and confidential sponsor or proposal documents

How to get help anyway Recommended

Do

  • Remove names, IDs and indirect identifiers (rare roles, dates, small groups), then use placeholders (Student A, Account 1). Data that seems anonymous can still identify a person.
  • Build the tool or template with made-up data, then fill in the real data yourself, outside AI
  • Ask AI about the structure: "Write a formula that totals column C by category"
  • Use aggregates instead of rows: totals by category, not lines by person
  • Keep the real file in UW storage (Box, OneDrive, Google Drive with NetID)

Don't

  • Upload a class roster or a Workday export with names and IDs
  • Paste a salary, payroll or reimbursement spreadsheet with names
  • Upload receipts that show card numbers or home addresses
  • Ask AI to draft a personnel or student-conduct decision
  • Assume a NetID login makes a personal account safe
  • Paste sensitive data into an approved tool without an approved risk assessment

Budgets

  • Department budget summaries and category totals are internal: UW NetID tools only.
  • Lines that identify a person (salary, payout, reimbursement to a named person) or contain account and banking details: keep them out, or replace them with placeholders.
  • Sponsor budgets in proposals that are not yet submitted are confidential: ask before using any AI tool.

Check before you use it

  • Compare every number, date, name and dollar amount with the source.
  • Check totals yourself; AI can add wrong and still sound sure.
  • Confirm policy statements against the official page. AI can invent rules and links.
  • Read the whole draft before you send it. Tone matters as much as facts.

What we can do with AI Recommended

Chatbots excel at text and data: reading, summarizing, organizing and checking. These kinds of tasks have been done in CBE, and they work. For each one, use a UW-approved tool and only the data allowed in it (see University policy), and have a person check the result.

Examples that already work

Assignments with many rulesTA, room or advisor assignments: courses, requests, preferences and rules checked all at once. Every rule break and open decision is flagged; a person makes the final calls.
Check documents, fill templatesLetters of recommendation, proposals, current & pending and biosketches: fill the template, then check every fact against the CV or the sponsor's rules.
Profiles from CVsOne-page faculty profiles built from long CVs, in PowerPoint and PDF, with every fact checked against the source.
Task navigatorsEverything you owe in one place, for yourself or a team. Paste an email or meeting notes and it suggests tasks, owners and dates.
Documents to websitesA long document, a book or a pile of files becomes a site people can click through, such as a guide to graduate student resources.
Spreadsheets to dashboardsStatic numbers become an interactive view with charts and filters. Use made-up or aggregated data.
A digital twin from photosOur distillation lab, rebuilt in 3D from a few phone photos: try the live twin.
Meeting notes to actionsRaw notes become decisions and action items with owners and dates.

Ideas we could build together

Student step-by-step navigatorsReimbursements, purchases and forms: each step links to the form, the rule and the person to ask.
Staff procedure navigatorsStreamline our procedures: Workday tasks, onboarding, room requests, visits.
3D renderings of our facilitiesTours and room or lab planning, from photos only. No scaled drawings or utilities data (UW-6060).
Lab equipment tutorialsStep by step, with photos and safety checks.
Seminar schedules and materialsSpeakers, rooms, announcements and flyers in one place.

What we have learned

  • Automate the tedious: text, documents and number crunching.
  • Make it easier to digest: better visuals for data and documents.
  • Keep track of tasks: for yourself and for a team.
  • Help with complex tasks: such as assignments.
  • It is an assistant, not autopilot: it needs your scrutiny. Check everything before you use it.

Have an idea for your area? Start with the task people ask about most, and talk with your supervisor about what data can go in.

Build your AI skills Recommended

Using AI well is a skill, and like any skill it takes practice. Try these with public or made-up data first.

Write a good prompt: five ingredients

ContextWho you are and what you're working on: "I coordinate travel reimbursements for an engineering department…"
TaskExactly what you want: summarize, draft, check, compare, or turn into a table.
ConstraintsLimits: "use only the policy text I pasted, under 150 words, friendly tone."
FormatHow to answer: "a checklist", "a table with columns Item, Required, Missing".
CheckAsk it to show its work: "list anything you weren't sure about."

Weak prompt

"Write an email about the travel report."

Strong prompt

"I process travel reimbursements. Draft a short, friendly email to a faculty member whose report is missing a hotel receipt and the conference agenda. Explain what is needed and the deadline (Dec 5). Under 120 words, no jargon."

Useful phrases

"Ask me questions before you start.""Use only the text I pasted.""Turn this into a checklist." "Make this shorter and friendlier.""Put this in a table.""What is missing from this document?" "List anything you are unsure about.""Explain it as you would to a new staff member.""Give me three options."

Practice tasks

📄 Policy to checklistPaste a public UW policy page and ask for a one-page checklist. Compare it with the original. Did it miss or invent anything?
📊 Numbers to pictureMake up a small budget-by-category table and ask for a chart and three takeaways. Check the totals yourself.
✉️ Draft and polishWrite a quick, rough email and ask for a clearer, friendlier version. Keep what sounds like you.
🗓️ Schedule a visitGive a made-up visitor's availability and a list of meetings, and ask for a day schedule. Check every time slot.
📝 Notes to actionsPaste meeting notes without names and ask for decisions, actions, owners and dates.
🔎 Catch the mistakeAsk AI a question about a UW procedure you know well. Where is it right, and where is it confidently wrong?

What to do if…

SituationWhat to do
You're not sure whether data is sensitiveTreat it as sensitive and ask your supervisor, or the Office of Cybersecurity.
You're not sure a tool is approvedCheck it.wisc.edu/ai. If it isn't listed, treat it as public. Questions: DoIT Help Desk, 608-264-4357.
A third-party AI bot (Otter, Read.ai, Fireflies) joins your meetingRemove it, and use Zoom or Webex AI instead. See how to protect meetings from AI bots.
You need AI for sensitive or restricted dataRequest a Cybersecurity review; it also needs your unit's risk executive (typically the dean).
A vendor or app turns on a new AI featureDon't use it with university data until you've checked with IT that it is approved.
You want AI to help with a recurring team processTalk with your supervisor first; agree on what data goes in and who checks the results.
You may have entered sensitive data into an AI toolStop, and tell your supervisor. Suspected data incidents must be reported: report it to the UW Office of Cybersecurity (urgent: DoIT Help Desk, 608-264-4357, option 3).

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms used in these guidelines.

Artificial intelligenceAny computer system doing tasks that need human-like intelligence
Machine learningLearns patterns from data
Deep learningMany-layered neural networks
Generative AICreates new text, images or code
Large language modelsText-based generative AI behind chatbots
Generative AI
AI that creates new content (text, tables, images, code) based on patterns learned from large amounts of data. Copilot, Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude are examples.
Large language model
The engine behind AI chatbots: a model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word. It produces fluent answers, but it doesn't check facts the way a person would.
AI tool vs. model
The model is the engine. The tool or product (a chat app, a plug-in) wraps it with an interface and data protections. The same model can be safe in one tool and unsafe in another.
Prompt
The question, instructions or material you type or upload into an AI tool. Treat a prompt in a consumer tool like a public post.
Hallucination
When AI states something false with confidence: wrong numbers, invented rules, fake links. This is why you check everything.
Artifact
A small interactive page an AI assistant builds for you, such as a dashboard or a task tracker, that you can open and use like a website.
AI agent
An AI system that can take actions on its own (send messages, edit files, fill forms) to reach a goal. Keep a person in charge of anything it sends or changes.
UW-approved tool
An AI service UW–Madison provides with a NetID login and contract protections for university data, such as Microsoft Copilot Chat or Google Gemini.
Consumer tool
A personal or free AI account (e.g., free ChatGPT or a personal Claude account). It has no UW data protections, even if you sign in with your NetID.
Data classification
UW's system for labeling data by risk (Public, Internal, Sensitive, Restricted; becoming Low, Moderate and High risk in 2027). It decides which data may go into which tools.
FERPA
The federal law that protects student education records, such as grades, ID numbers and advising notes.
De-identify
Remove or replace names, ID numbers and other details that point to a person before you use data.
