    User guide

    A 1600 km natural gas pipeline from a research model (Zavala, 2014), with 10 compressor stations and 7 customers. Everything you see is computed live.

    Disclaimer: This is for educational purposes only. This digital twin is based on a combination of public data, scientific publications and fictionalized settings.

    • Drag to orbit · scroll or pinch to zoom · right-drag to pan.
    • Camera buttons, bottom right: Overview, Station, Inside pipe, River span, Delivery.

      Each home is one heat balance (CBE 250, 320): heat stored = furnace heat − heat lost through walls, windows and air leaks. The values are typical for a single-family home, assumed for this twin. The time constant is why a house cools slowly when the heat stops (CBE 470). In steady state the furnace must replace the loss, , so gas use grows with the difference between inside and outside: at 0 °C a 21 °C home needs 5.3 kW; at −30 °C it needs 12.8 kW.

      One degree on all 110,000 thermostats: of heat, or of gas = 0.077 MMSCM/d. A night setback saves gas overnight, but every furnace runs flat out when people wake up (cold-load pickup), a sharp morning peak the pipeline has to supply from line pack.

      Plants: fuel = electric output ÷ efficiency (assumed 45 % for the cogeneration plant, 31 % for the steam station, 30 % for the turbines); 1 MW of fuel = 2560 standard m³ of gas per day.

      Real world: on 30 January 2019, during a polar vortex, a fire at Consumers Energy's Ray compressor station in Michigan cut supply and the utility asked customers to set thermostats to 65 °F (Utility Dive, NPR). Madison plant sizes: MGE.

      Real world

      Why gas pipelines matter

      A steel pipe as wide as a doorway, buried for hundreds of kilometres; compressor stations keep the gas moving.

      • Heat: the main heating fuel in about 45 % of US homes.
      • Power: the largest single source of US electricity.
      • Products: fertilizer and chemicals.

      About 300,000 miles of US transmission lines. The trade-offs: reliable winter heat, CO₂ and methane leaks, safety, and a possible future for hydrogen and CO₂.

      Chemical engineers design the stations, model the line and run the plants at its ends.

        About this digital twin

        Model: Zavala (2014), checked against an independent Python solver; customers and equipment added for teaching. An integrative case study for UW–Madison CBE students.

        Model and validation

        Isothermal flow in each link, written in mass flow $f$ and pressure $p$ as in Zavala (2014), eqs. (2.6):

        Discretised exactly as eqs. (3.11): 10 points per link, implicit Euler in time, node balances, link boundary conditions, compressors at the inlet of active links adding a boost $\Delta\theta$, and power $P = c_4 f_{\mathrm{in}}\,[((\theta+\Delta\theta)/\theta)^{\beta} - 1]$. Every step solves the whole network (240 unknowns) with Newton's method.

        • Network of Fig. 1: 13 nodes, 12 links, compressors at nodes 2 to 11, supply at node 1 fixed at 57 bar, demand at node 13. $D$ = 914 mm, 100 km between compressors; the two end links share the remaining 600 km (300 km each, assumed).
        • Appendix A gas data: $c_p$ = 2.34, $c_v$ = 1.85 kJ/kg K, $M$ = 18 kg/kmol, $T$ = 293.15 K, $\rho_{\mathrm{n}}$ = 0.72 kg/m³. Assumed $z$ = 0.8 and rugosity 0.012 mm, giving $c$ = 370 m/s and $\lambda$ = 0.0084.
        • Demand nominal 10 MMSCM/d with steps to 11, 12 or 13 MMSCM/d for 5 h from 9.5 h (Section 5). Limits: delivery ≥ 39 bar, suction ≥ 34 bar, power ≤ 3000 kW.
        • Set-point mode closes the loop on $\Delta\theta$ (the practical setting noted in Section 3), with $\Delta\theta \ge 0$ and $P \le$ 3000 kW enforced. If the network cannot carry the demand, the model curtails it, since the paper treats demands as soft.

        Validation: this JavaScript solver was checked against an independent Python implementation of eqs. (3.11) solved with MINPACK. Over 24 h with the high-demand step, boost, set-point and power-limited stations, the two agree to 3·10⁻¹⁴ bar and 7·10⁻¹³ kg/s at $\Delta\tau$ = 1800 s, and to 1·10⁻¹³ bar at this model's 300 s step.

        Gas particles each carry a fixed mass and move with the local gas velocity $v = f c^2/(pA)$, so their spacing shows density: after each compressor the gas is denser and slower.
