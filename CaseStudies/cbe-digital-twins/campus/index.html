    User guide

    A 3D model of how a big university heats, cools and powers itself, and what cutting its CO₂ would cost. Every number comes from an hour-by-hour model of one year.

    How to explore

    • Opens on the Campus overview with the numbered ChemE spots.
    • Click a spot (or its row on the left) to zoom in and open its card.
    • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom.
    • Camera buttons, bottom right: Campus (overview), Lakeshore, Plants, West campus, Overhead.

    Buttons

    • Energy tour: 13 guided stops through plants, pipes and buildings.
    • Networks: shows the steam and chilled-water pipes, with flow.
    • Heat map: colours buildings by this hour's heating or cooling demand.
    • Day/night lighting: sun, sky and lights follow the clock; off holds midday light.
    • Real world: what this system is and why it matters.
    • Conditions: live readings on the plants and landmarks.
    • Subject chips (Balances, Thermo, …): a short lesson per course.
    • Winter · Spring · Summer · Fall and Day · Night: jump in time. Play and the speed buttons run the clock.

    Change conditions

    • Pick a Pathway in the side panel, or move the levers under Electricity, Heating, Cooling and Economics.
    • Watch annual CO₂, cost, and the "What this means" boxes.

    Equations, courses, problems

    • ∑ Model: all 26 equations with symbols and live values.
    • Links to Courses: course map, problem sets and the integrative problem.
    • The side panel's ⤢ pops it out as a window.

      Real world

      Heating, cooling and powering a campus

      A university runs like a small city: central plants make steam and cold water for hundreds of buildings, and electricity comes from the grid.118

      Why it matters

      Buildings use 36.9 % of US energy.9 Cleaner power, heat pumps, storage and new fuels can cut CO₂, each at a cost.

      Where chemical engineers fit

      They design plants, heat exchangers, storage, batteries and capture solvents, and model cost against carbon.

          About this digital twin

          An outreach model based on the CBE 450 Process Design case study.18 All numbers are computed in your browser.

          One process, many courses: an integrative case study for UW–Madison CBE students.

          Model, assumptions and sources

          Every number on this page comes from a model running in your browser. In the tour, Case data marks values from the CBE 450 case-study data sheet and public facts about the campus, Typical marks literature or industry ranges, and Assumed marks choices made for this model.

          • Demands. From the case-study data sheet: 402,637 MWh/yr of building electricity, 1.04 Mt/yr of steam at about 2.0 GJ/t (≈ 579 GWh of heat) and 24 Mt/yr of chilled water at 60 kJ/kg (≈ 406 GWh). Chiller electricity is added on top of building electricity, as in the published case study.
          • Weather. One synthetic Madison year (seeded): seasonal and daily temperature cycles with autocorrelated weather swings (low ≈ −22 °C, high ≈ 32 °C), Stull wet bulb, clear-sky sun with daily cloud, a Rayleigh wind series. PV and wind output are scaled to 14.5 % and 36 % capacity factors.
          • Loads. Heat = base (≈ 30 MW: reheat, hot water, labs) + lumped UA below an 18 °C balance point; cooling = 26 MW lab and data-centre base + degree-hours above 12 °C + solar; electricity follows occupancy and heat. All three are scaled to the annual totals.
          • Dispatch, every hour. Heat: heat-recovery heat pumps (limited by the coincident cooling load), ground-source (limited by 45 W/m of bore), hot-water storage, air-source (above −20 °C), CHP, then boilers. Cooling: heat-recovery by-product, chilled storage (discharge 11:00–19:00, charge at night), ground-coupled cooling, then electric or steam-driven chillers. Electricity: on-site and contracted generation, then a battery that shaves each day's peaks, then the grid. Surplus is exported at $30/MWh with no carbon credit.
          • Physics. Heat-pump and chiller COP from Carnot fractions with approach temperatures; boiler efficiency 85 % (steam) to 94 % (condensing at 25 °C return); network losses by cylindrical conduction through insulation, × 1.8 for traps and aging on steam; pumping from flow and pressure drop; borefield drift through a long-term g-function; capture regeneration steam solved as a fixed point on boiler fuel.
          • Emissions. Natural gas 0.181 t/MWh (HHV), renewable gas 0.07, gasoline 8.89 kg/gal, grid 0.56 t/MWh today (slider). Location-based, annual-average grid factor. Grid CO₂ is shared among uses in proportion to their electricity each hour. Transport = the fleet (data sheet) plus a fixed remainder of the 36 kt case-study figure for commuting and other travel. The model baseline is about 450 kt, against the case-study estimate of ≈ 500 kt.
          • Calibration. Building demands are the case data. Three plant parameters were then fitted to the FY2024 state energy report for UW–Madison: steam-boiler efficiency 0.82 → 0.85, steam-chiller COP 0.8 → 1.2 (mostly steam-turbine-driven chillers), and the electric share of cooling 35 % → 50 %. Electricity and heating-plant fuel now match the state figures within a few per cent.
          • Economics. Capital annualized with each asset's life at the chosen discount rate; NPV over 25 years against the current campus; electricity $99.8/MWh + $15/kW-month demand charge; gas $5/MMBtu; RNG $40/MMBtu; gasoline $3.10/gal. Costs of solar, wind, microreactor, heat pumps, chillers, batteries and storage follow a 2025 published campus case study; capture costs and electrolysis follow values used in the class. Steam-to-hot-water conversion ($250M network + $60/m² of buildings) and retrofit costs are assumptions. Replacing aging boilers and steam lines, which today's campus would also have to pay for, is not credited.
          • What is simplified. One weather year, no demand growth, no hourly grid emission factor, no learning curves, no uncertainty analysis (which the final report asked for), a single aggregated boiler fleet, and an existing cogeneration plant whose electricity is part of the grid.

          Map and buildings: footprints and names of 319 university buildings from the official UW–Madison campus map (map.wisc.edu); floor counts for 69 of them, about 4,100 other buildings, Lakes Mendota, Monona and Wingra, the streets and the Howard Temin Lakeshore Path from OpenStreetMap (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL). Floor area = footprint × floors. Building energy: Midwest energy intensity by building type from the EIA Commercial Buildings Energy Consumption Survey 2018 (Table C5), with laboratories at 4× an office (labs typically use 3–10× more per m²); campus totals from the Wisconsin PSC FY2021 Energy Use in State Facilities report. Utility tunnel routes are not public, so the networks follow main streets.

          Sources: the CBE 450 Spring 2026 case-study handouts and data sheet; Ind. Eng. Chem. Res. 2024, 63, 2276 (graph-based technology pathways for a campus); Sustainable Energy, Grids and Networks 44 (2025) 101996 (multi-scale campus energy transition); public descriptions of the campus heating plants and the cogeneration facility; EPA emission factors. The scenario set and the cost-vs-emissions conclusions summarise the class's findings in aggregate; no student work is reproduced.
